title: Ethical and Moral Limits on High-Fidelity Ancestor Simulation Could Tighten With Distance From the Simulators' Era
description: Why an ethically constrained descendant civilization might be more willing to simulate eras close to its own, and what that implies about whether we are simulated.

A technologically advanced descendant civilization capable of simulating a world like ours in sufficient detail (i.e. subjective experience and capability to experience pain and suffering in a manner similar or functionally identical to us) would, more likely than not, place ethical and moral limits on doing so (whether institutional or otherwise), and those limits or possible objectionability could potentially increase the further the simulated era is from the simulators' own.

Imagine a hypothetical where our own civilization is given only the capability to run high-fidelity simulations far beyond current capabilities, enough so that we could run whole-brain simulations with physiological and subjective experience similar to or functionally identical to ours. Intense debate and institutional caution would follow, as has been shown to occur with debates around embryo research and, more recently, the possible moral status of large language models. If we suppose the technology could only simulate a period far enough back to be significantly divorced from the present, such as 1,000 or 2,000 years ago, then institutions and philosophers could potentially object on the simple assertion that the simulation would create unnecessary pain and suffering.

However, suffering alone would not be the sole consideration; the present day contains a great deal of nonconsensual and unnecessary suffering. Most people would not say that the formation of the universe was worse than nothing at all, even though the universe contains a great deal of suffering and nothing contains none. That intuition is about a world that already exists, and a civilization deciding whether to create a simulated one faces a harder choice, since it would be responsible for the suffering it brings about.

A civilization considering a simulation would therefore weigh a second factor: how far the simulated conditions fall below its own standard of living. A sufficiently high-fidelity simulation of a distant past could reproduce or produce individuals with subjective experience and thus, the capacity to experience scarcity, disease, and violence that would increase in relative objectionability as the time between the simulator’s present and the simulated past increases. Most present-day individuals, if asked whether they would run a fully conscious simulation of distant eras featuring significant suffering (such as say, Plague-Era Europe), would probably have some degree of ethical and moral considerations and might hesitate or refuse; however, they would likely hesitate or object less over a period closer to their own.

A sufficiently advanced civilization could therefore weigh population-ethical thresholds; if its own floor for standard of living is far higher than ours, a simulation of our era would contain a large amount of suffering below the threshold that it would consider acceptable. A simulation of an era closer to theirs, that potentially could be closer in similar material abundance and similar ability to prevent suffering, would be easier to justify. Permissibility would then vary with the distance between the simulated era and the simulating one.

This is hard to weigh against other considerations when estimating whether one is in a simulation, but it could be included. If ethical constraints shape which simulations end up getting run, the simulated observers who exist could potentially cluster in eras close or closer than otherwise to the simulators' own, and in worlds with a reasonable floor of living and comfort; this could also coincide with aforementioned ethical and moral limits pushing a hypothetical descendant civilization towards avoiding partial simulations that focus on times of crisis or historical importance which may contain more suffering. Our world contains large amounts of severe suffering, which counts as potential evidence against being in an ancestor simulation[^bostrom] run by an ethically constrained descendant civilization.

This conclusion, evidently, only really applies to simulators whose ethics have developed roughly in the direction ours have (i.e. fairness, harm reduction, individual consent, and other relevant ideals), toward counting the suffering of simulated people and raising the floor below which they find it unacceptable. A civilization with no such constraint, or with values unlike ours, would face none of these limits.

[^bostrom]: Nick Bostrom, "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" *Philosophical Quarterly* 53, no. 211 (2003): 243–255. <https://simulation-argument.com/simulation.pdf>
